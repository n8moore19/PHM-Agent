Purpose A catalogued, cited inventory of the frameworks relevant to the PHM learning tool, spanning both what learners learn about (PHM content, Part A) and how the tool teaches (learning design, Part B).

Part A — PHM & Population-Health Content Frameworks
What the learner is being trained to understand.
A1. The Quintuple Aim
[Southlake-core]    Web-verified
What it is: The goal-set a PHM system is trying to achieve: five outcomes taken together, being improved population health, enhanced patient and care experience, reduced per-capita cost, improved care-team well-being, and advancing health equity.
Lineage: Triple Aim (Berwick, Nolan & Whittington, 2008), then Quadruple Aim adding clinician well-being (Bodenheimer & Sinsky, 2014), then Quintuple Aim adding health equity (Nundy, Cooper & Mate, 2022). Worth teaching as a story.
Relevance to the tool: The “why” layer, the outcomes every PHM action ladders up to. A natural spine for the “what this means for me” framing, since each role contributes to different aims.
Source: Nundy S, Cooper LA, Mate KS. The Quintuple Aim for Health Care Improvement: A New Imperative to Advance Health Equity. JAMA. 2022;327(6):521–522.
Link: JAMA article   ·   IHI overview

A2. The 5 Steps of PHM (PHM Cycle)
[Southlake-core]    From supplied docs   ⚠ Confirm Southlake's “5 Steps” match the WHO five; naming or count may differ.
What it is: The operational process of doing PHM, as a repeating cycle. The WHO version, our working scaffold, runs: (1) define and identify the population; (2) health assessment and population segmentation; (3) risk stratification and impactibility; (4) tailored service delivery; (5) evaluation and improvement.
Relevance to the tool: The “how” layer, the backbone learners walk through. Most persona questions (“what do I actually do?”) map onto a step.
Source: WHO Regional Office for Europe. Population health management in primary health care: a proactive approach to improve health and well-being. Copenhagen; 2023. (Supplied by Southlake.)
Link: WHO publication

A3. The 6 Domains of PHM
[Southlake-core]    From supplied docs   ⚠ Confirm against Southlake's own “6 Domains”; the count matches but names may not.
What it is: The enabling conditions PHM needs to function, the organisational and system scaffolding. Working scaffold is van Ede's six elements of PHM (from the Dutch PLOTmodel): accountable regional organisation, cross-domain business model, integrated data infrastructure, co-designing workforce and community, population health data analytics, and emergent implementation strategy.
Relevance to the tool: The “what has to be in place” layer. It answers administrator and leadership persona questions better than the 5 Steps do.
Note: van Ede's six elements are authored by M.A. Bruijnzeels, the same author who co-wrote the WHO PHM paper Southlake supplied. If Southlake's “6 Domains” derive from this lineage, two of their three core frameworks trace to peer-reviewed origins. Worth confirming.
Source: van Ede AFTM, Minderhout RN, Stein KV, Bruijnzeels MA. How to successfully implement population health management: a scoping review. BMC Health Serv Res. 2023;23:910. (Supplied by Southlake.)
Link: DOI 10.1186/s12913-023-09915-5

A4. The California Statewide Approach (CalAIM PHM)
[Southlake-core]    Web-verified
What it is: California's Department of Health Care Services (DHCS) built a cohesive, statewide PHM program as a cornerstone of its CalAIM Medi-Cal reform (launched 2023). It requires managed-care plans to run a whole-person, data-driven approach: member engagement, data-driven risk stratification and predictive analytics, upstream links to public health and social services, care management, coordination and transitions, and mitigation of social drivers of health, measured against NCQA plus DHCS PHM standards.
Relevance to the tool: The real-world, government-scale worked example. Useful as a case study showing PHM implemented as policy; its PHM Strategy and Roadmap is a concrete artifact learners can be pointed to.
Source: California DHCS. CalAIM Population Health Management. Final PHM Strategy and Roadmap (2022); program launched 2023.
Link: DHCS CalAIM PHM

A5. Rainbow Model of Integrated Care (RMIC)
[Proposed addition]    Web-verified
What it is: A model of integrated care across four levels (clinical, professional, organisational, system) and two integration types (normative, meaning shared values and culture; functional, meaning shared processes and data). The lens van Ede uses to locate where in the system each PHM action happens.
Relevance to the tool: Gives learners a clean way to see “at what level does my role operate?”, pairing naturally with the persona structure. It is also the exact structure the WHO paper uses for its system, organisational and clinical success factors, so it bridges A2 and A3.
Source: Valentijn PP, Schepman SM, Opheij W, Bruijnzeels MA. Understanding integrated care: a comprehensive conceptual framework based on the integrative functions of primary care. Int J Integr Care. 2013;13:e010.
Link: DOI 10.5334/ijic.886

A6. Context–Mechanism–Outcome (CMO)
[Proposed addition]    Web-verified
What it is: A realist-evaluation lens: what works, for whom, under what circumstances. Context (facilitators and barriers) plus Mechanism (how an intervention produces its effect) leads to Outcome.
Relevance to the tool: Less a learner-facing concept, more a design tool for us and for the Dialogue Bank; it structures case scenarios (“in this context, this action, this result”). Could be an advanced-learner module rather than a beginner concept.
Source: Referenced throughout van Ede; foundational realist-synthesis standard: Wong G, Greenhalgh T, Westhorp G, Buckingham J, Pawson R. RAMESES publication standards: realist syntheses. BMC Med. 2013;11:21.
Link: DOI 10.1186/1741-7015-11-21

A7. The Kaiser Pyramid (risk-stratified care model)
[Proposed addition]    From supplied docs
What it is: A triangle that sorts a population by risk into tiers: the general population (health promotion and prevention) at the base, high-risk patients (disease management) in the middle, and complex or high-need patients (case management) at the top, with the balance shifting from self-care toward professional care as you move up.
Relevance to the tool: The single most intuitive visual for the “tailored service delivery” step and for the segmentation and stratification concepts. A strong candidate for one of the requested diagrams, and it connects directly to Southlake's own tiering work.
Source: Described in the WHO paper (adapted from Kaiser Permanente). WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2023 (as A2).
Link: WHO publication

A8. Social Determinants of Health (SDOH)
[Proposed addition]    From supplied docs
What it is: The non-clinical conditions (income, housing, education, environment, social support) that drive the majority of health outcomes. The WHO paper's striking framing: health-care systems determine only about 10% of health outcomes, while behaviour and social or physical settings determine at least about 50%.
Relevance to the tool: The concept that reframes PHM from “medicine” to “population health.” Nearly every persona needs it, and it is the through-line connecting PHM to the equity aim (A1) and to proportionate universalism (A9).
Source: WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2023 (as A2); concept originates with the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health.
Link: WHO publication

A9. Proportionate Universalism
[Proposed addition]    From supplied docs
What it is: Delivering services universally but at a scale and intensity proportionate to need, reducing the social gradient in health without a purely targeted (and potentially stigmatising) approach.
Relevance to the tool: Answers “how do we help everyone but focus effort where it is needed?”, the philosophical bridge between population-level and individual-level action, which is exactly the Population Health versus Population Health Management distinction the SoW makes central.
Source: WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2023 (as A2); originates with the Marmot Review, Fair Society, Healthy Lives, 2010.
Link: WHO publication


Part B — Learning-Design & Instructional Frameworks
How the tool should teach, directly relevant to the gating mechanism, champion pathway, and repeat-use goals in the SoW.
B1. Andragogy — Adult Learning Theory
[Proposed addition]    Web-verified
What it is: Knowles' theory of how adults learn differently from children, built on self-direction, drawing on prior experience, readiness to learn, a problem-centred (not subject-centred) orientation, intrinsic motivation, and a need to know why.
Relevance to the tool: The foundational justification for the entire design. Learners are working professionals with varied prior knowledge, so andragogy is why the gating mechanism (meet them at their level), the “what this means for me” framing (relevance), and the case and dialogue approach (problem-centred) are the right choices. The most defensible pedagogy anchor to cite.
Source: Knowles MS. The Adult Learner. Foundational works, 1968–1984.
Link: Adult Learning Theory overview

B2. Bloom's Taxonomy (Revised)
[Proposed addition]    Web-verified
What it is: A hierarchy of cognitive complexity: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create. Revised by Anderson and Krathwohl (2001) from nouns to active verbs, with a second “knowledge type” dimension.
Relevance to the tool: The scaffold for the gating mechanism and knowledge-check gate. It lets us define what “novice” versus “well-versed” actually means in measurable terms (a novice remembers and understands the 5 Steps; a champion applies and evaluates them in their role), giving the diagnostic gate a principled way to route learners.
Source: Anderson LW, Krathwohl DR (Eds.). A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives. Longman; 2001. (Orig. Bloom, 1956.)
Link: UIC teaching guide

B3. Competency-Based Education (CBME) / Mastery Learning
[Proposed addition]    Web-verified
What it is: An outcomes-based approach in which learners progress by demonstrating mastery of a defined competency rather than by time served; advancement is gated on proven ability.
Relevance to the tool: The conceptual backbone of the two-stage Champion Pathway. Stage 1 (become PHM-trained), then a gate, then Stage 2 (demonstrate real-world application) is textbook competency-based progression. Naming it gives the champion process a recognised pedigree.
Source: Frank JR, Mungroo R, Ahmad Y, Wang M, De Rossi S, Horsley T. Toward a definition of competency-based education in medicine: a systematic review of published definitions. Med Teach. 2010;32(8):631–637.
Link: DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2010.500898


How These Frameworks Stack
Draft architecture — pending validation in the Framework & Landscape Synthesis deliverable.
The three Southlake-core content frameworks are not competitors; they are three layers of one picture, and the proposed additions slot into the same stack. This layering is a working hypothesis to react to, not a settled decision.
WHY   Quintuple Aim (A1)
The five outcomes everything ladders up to.
▲
HOW   The 5 Steps / PHM Cycle (A2)
define → assess & segment → stratify → deliver → evaluate  (repeats)
The Kaiser Pyramid (A7) sits inside “deliver.”
▲
ENABLE   The 6 Domains (A3)
The organisational and system conditions that make it work.
Mapped across the RMIC levels (A5).

Cross-cutting throughout: SDOH (A8) and Proportionate Universalism (A9), with the CalAIM approach (A4) as the real-world worked example of all three layers.
Taught via: Andragogy (B1), gated by Bloom's Taxonomy (B2), progressed by Competency-Based mastery (B3).
