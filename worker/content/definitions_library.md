# PHM Concept and Definitions Library (Southlake)

Shared vocabulary for Population Health Management at Southlake. Each concept is presented at three levels: formal definition, plain language, what it means for the learner's role. Anchor IDs in [brackets] are the Master Reroute Anchor IDs cited by the dialogue banks.

## Foundational concepts

### Population health [POP-vs-PHM]
Definition: The health outcomes of a defined group of people, including how those outcomes are distributed across the group.
Plain language: Population health looks at how healthy a group or community is overall and whether some groups experience better or worse outcomes than others.
Southlake example: Examining differences in diabetes outcomes, hospital use or access to primary care across communities in Southlake's region.

### Population Health Management (PHM) [POP-vs-PHM]
Definition: A people-centred, data-driven and proactive approach to improving the health and well-being of a defined population by understanding differences within that population and providing services according to need.
Plain language: PHM means understanding who is in a population, identifying what different people need, acting earlier and coordinating the right support.
What makes it different: Population health describes the outcomes Southlake wants to improve. PHM is the organized process Southlake uses to improve them.

### Public health
Definition: The organized efforts of society to protect and improve health through prevention, promotion, policy, surveillance and community-level intervention.
Plain language: Public health typically works across entire communities through initiatives such as vaccination, health promotion, disease surveillance and policy.
Relationship to PHM: Public health and PHM overlap, but PHM connects community-level understanding to proactive support for identified groups and individuals.

### Defined population [DEFINED-POP]
Definition: A clearly identified group for which an organization or partnership accepts responsibility.
May be based on: geography; attachment to a provider or Ontario Health Team; a shared condition; demographic characteristics; a common health or social need.
Example: All residents within the Southlake Community Ontario Health Team's attributed population, or a subgroup of residents living with diabetes.

### Proactive care [PROACTIVE]
Definition: Identifying and addressing potential needs before a person experiences an avoidable crisis or independently seeks care.
Plain language: Instead of waiting for someone to arrive at the emergency department, the system uses available information to recognize need and offer support earlier.

## The goal of PHM

### Quintuple Aim [QUINTUPLE]
Five outcomes health-system improvement should pursue together:
1. Improved population health
2. Better patient and care experience
3. Lower per-capita cost
4. Improved care-team well-being
5. Greater health equity
Plain language: PHM should produce healthier communities, better experiences, sustainable costs, healthier care teams and fairer outcomes.
Important point: An intervention should not improve one aim by seriously harming another. For example, reducing costs should not come at the expense of patient experience or equity.

### Health equity [EQUITY/SDOH]
Definition: The absence of avoidable, unfair or unjust differences in health outcomes and access to the conditions needed for good health.
Plain language: Everyone should have a fair opportunity to be healthy, recognizing that some people face greater barriers and may need different or additional support.

### Social determinants of health [EQUITY/SDOH]
Definition: The non-medical conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age that influence health.
Include: income and employment; housing; food security; education; transportation; social support; physical environment; experiences of discrimination.
PHM relevance: Clinical information alone does not provide a complete understanding of a person's health or their ability to follow a care plan.

### Proportionate universalism [EQUITY/SDOH]
Definition: Providing support to everyone while increasing the scale or intensity of support according to the level of need.
Plain language: Everyone receives a foundation of support, but people facing greater barriers receive more help.
Example: All patients receive preventive-health information, while individuals facing food insecurity or transportation barriers receive additional navigation and community support.

## The five-step PHM cycle [CYCLE]
PHM should be understood as a repeating learning cycle, not a one-time project.

### Step 1: Define and identify the population [DEFINED-POP]
Definition: Establishing the population for which the organization or partnership is responsible and identifying the individuals included within it.
Question: Who are we responsible for?
Example: Identifying everyone within an attributed geographic population who is living with Type 2 diabetes.

### Step 2: Health assessment and population segmentation [SEGMENT]
Definition: Understanding the population's health and social needs, then dividing it into groups with similar characteristics or needs.
Question: What do people need, and how do their needs differ?
Example: Separating people living with diabetes into groups based on factors such as disease stability, mental health, food security and access to primary care.

### Population segmentation [SEGMENT]
Definition: Dividing a population into meaningful groups whose members have similar needs, characteristics or patterns of service use.
Important distinction: Segmentation describes different kinds of need. It is not necessarily a ranking from "better" to "worse."

### Step 3: Risk stratification and impactibility [RISK-IMPACT]
Definition: Assessing the likelihood of future adverse outcomes and the extent to which available interventions may improve those outcomes.
Question: Who is at risk, and who could benefit from action?

### Risk stratification [RISK-IMPACT]
Definition: Grouping people according to their likelihood of experiencing an outcome such as hospitalization, deterioration or avoidable service use.
Important point: A risk level should inform professional judgment, not replace it.

### Impactibility [RISK-IMPACT]
Definition: The likelihood that a person or group will benefit from a particular intervention.
Plain language: Risk asks, "Who may experience a problem?" Impactibility asks, "Where can our actions make a meaningful difference?" Someone may be high risk but unlikely to benefit from one intervention, while a medium-risk person may benefit greatly from early support.

### Step 4: Tailored service delivery [TAILORED]
Definition: Designing and providing services according to the needs, preferences, risks and circumstances of different groups and individuals.
Question: What support is appropriate for this person or group?
Possible responses: prevention and health promotion; self-management support; primary-care connection; disease management; care coordination; intensive case management; community and social supports.

### Step 5: Evaluation and improvement [EVAL-LOOP]
Definition: Monitoring implementation and outcomes, learning from results and adjusting the approach.
Question: Did it work, for whom, and what should change?
Evaluation may consider: access to care; patient and provider experience; service use; health outcomes; equity; cost; referral completion; unintended consequences.
The cycle then begins again using what was learned.

## The six enabling domains [DOMAINS]
The five steps explain how PHM is carried out. The six domains explain what must be in place for that process to work.

- Accountable regional organization: A partnership or organization accepts responsibility for improving outcomes across a defined population. Plain language: Someone must be answerable for how well the region is doing, rather than every organization focusing only on its own services.
- Cross-domain business model: Funding, costs, benefits and incentives are considered across health and social-care organizations. Plain language: Organizations need incentives that support prevention and collaboration, even when one organization makes the investment and another experiences the savings.
- Integrated data infrastructure [INT-DATA]: Health and social information can be appropriately connected and shared to create a more complete view of individuals and populations. Plain language: Relevant information should move with the person instead of remaining trapped in separate systems.
- Co-designing workforce and community: Providers, staff, patients and community members meaningfully participate in designing services and making decisions. Plain language: PHM should be designed with the people delivering and receiving support, not simply for them.
- Population health data analytics: Data is converted into information that helps identify needs, guide decisions and evaluate outcomes. Plain language: Collecting data is not enough. People need to be able to understand it and act on it.
- Emergent implementation strategy: PHM is implemented through continuous testing, learning and adaptation. Plain language: There is no perfect implementation blueprint. Teams try approaches, learn from results and adjust as needs and conditions change.

## Supporting concepts

### Integrated care
Definition: Care coordinated across people, professions, organizations and settings to provide a continuous and person-centred experience.
Levels: Clinical (coordination around an individual); Professional (collaboration among different professions); Organizational (collaboration among organizations); System (policies and structures that support coordination).

### Co-design
Definition: A collaborative process in which people affected by a service participate meaningfully in its design, implementation and evaluation.
Important distinction: Asking for feedback after designing a solution is consultation. Co-design involves people early enough to influence decisions.

### Person-centred care
Definition: Care that respects and responds to an individual's needs, preferences, goals, values and circumstances.
PHM relationship: PHM uses population-level information, but its interventions should still recognize the individual rather than treating everyone in a segment identically.

### Tailored intervention [TAILORED]
Definition: A service or action adapted to the needs and circumstances of a particular population segment or individual.
Tailoring could change: type of service; intensity; timing; location; language; delivery channel; provider; level of follow-up.

### Closed-loop referral [CLOSED-LOOP]
Definition: A referral process in which the organization confirms whether the person successfully connected with and received the intended service.
Plain language: The process does not end when a referral is sent. Someone checks whether the person actually received help and responds if the connection failed.

### Continuous learning [EVAL-LOOP]
Definition: Regularly using data, experience and feedback to improve how PHM is designed and delivered. Includes learning from unsuccessful interventions, barriers and unintended outcomes, not only successful pilots.

## Appendix A: California (CalAIM) concepts, a worked example, not a model to copy
- Local Planning and PHM Strategy: Health plans work with public-health agencies and communities to identify priorities, set shared goals and report progress.
- Gathering Member Information: Plans combine clinical, demographic, behavioural and social information to understand individual needs and preferences.
- Risk Stratification, Segmentation and Tiering: Members assigned to low, medium-rising or high-risk categories to support outreach, assessment and service planning.
- Basic Population Health Management: Preventive care, health education, primary-care engagement and basic coordination available across the population, not only to high-risk members.
- Complex Care Management: Additional coordination for members with high or rising clinical or social needs.
- Enhanced Care Management: Intensive, person-centred coordination for members with the most complex needs, often led by a designated care manager.
- Transitional Care Services: Support when someone moves between settings or levels of care, such as leaving hospital and returning home.
- Community Supports: Non-clinical services addressing health-related social needs, such as housing navigation, medically tailored meals and home-based environmental supports.
(Ontario translation: "member" means a resident or patient in the OHT's attributed population.)

## Appendix B: How the concepts connect (worked example)
A regional analysis shows that people living with diabetes in one community experience higher hospital use.
1. Population health identifies the unequal outcome.
2. Population definition identifies the affected community.
3. Assessment and segmentation reveal different clinical and social needs.
4. Risk stratification identifies people at increased risk of hospitalization.
5. Impactibility considers who may benefit from outreach.
6. Tailored delivery connects people to primary care, diabetes education, mental-health support or food services.
7. Closed-loop referrals confirm that people receive the support.
8. Evaluation examines health outcomes, hospital use, experience and equity.
9. Continuous learning uses the results to improve the next cycle.
