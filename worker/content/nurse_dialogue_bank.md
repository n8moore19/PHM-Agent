1.  The frameworks a nurse lives inside
Five frameworks carry the nurse’s questions. Concepts themselves are defined in the Master Reroute Anchor Library; here they are shown as pictures with the nurse’s position marked.
1.1  The five-step PHM cycle: the backbone

Figure 1. The five-step PHM cycle. Orange = steps where the nurse acts; teal = a step the nurse feeds but does not run.
What to see: the loop runs clockwise from Step 1. The nurse is orange on four of the five steps. At Step 1–2 (top and right) the nurse is the origin of the record, the assessment and charting that define and describe the population. At Step 4 (bottom left) the nurse delivers tailored care. At Step 5 (left) the nurse generates the did-it-work signals evaluation runs on. Step 3 (bottom right) is teal, risk stratification is usually done for the nurse, but they receive its output to prioritise.
Where the questions live: Q1.1 and Q2.3 (charting) sit at Steps 1–2, they are about the quality of the record the whole loop depends on. Q1.3 (frequent-flyer) is the loop failing to turn: the person re-enters at the top instead of being caught. Q2.1 (floating) is about reading Step 3’s output. Q3.6 (did it work) is Step 5.
1.2  Levels of integrated care: where the nurse operates

Figure 2. The levels of integrated care. The nurse works at the clinical / micro level and feeds upward.
What to see: four stacked levels. The nurse is the orange band at the bottom, clinical / micro, care around one person. The upward orange arrow shows the nurse’s charting and handoffs feeding the professional and organisational (meso) levels above. The nurse rarely acts at the system/macro level, but their data ends up there.
Where the questions live: Q3.3 (who do I collaborate with, before/after my part) is the micro→meso arrow itself. Q3.1 (what do I contribute) is the micro band feeding everything above it.

1.3  Risk-stratified care: the nurse at every tier

Figure 3. The Kaiser pyramid. The nurse (orange callouts) acts at every tier, but the work differs by tier.
What to see: three tiers by need. The orange callouts on the left show the nurse’s work changing as you climb: teach and screen across the ~70–80% general population at the base; monitor and catch deterioration for the ~15% high-risk middle; intensive follow-up and coordination for the ~5% complex tip. The self-care ↔ professional-care scale beneath the pyramid shows that same shift as need rises.
Where the questions live: Q2.2 (risk vs impactibility) is the whole pyramid, impactibility asks not just which tier someone is in, but where the nurse’s action changes the outcome most. Q3.4 (“I can’t do everything with these ratios”) is about deliberately aiming scarce nursing time up this pyramid instead of spreading it flat.
1.4  The Quintuple Aim: the “why”

Figure 4. The Quintuple Aim. Orange = the aims a nurse most directly moves.
What to see: five aims pursued together. Three are orange, the ones a nurse most directly shapes: care experience (every encounter), health equity (who gets reached), and care-team well-being (the nurse’s own sustainability). Population health and cost are greyed, the nurse contributes, but less directly.
Where the questions live: Q3.5 (could my work reinforce inequity) sits on the equity node. Q1.4 and Q3.4 (workload, ratios) sit on care-team well-being — the aim that makes the nurse’s own strain a first-class PHM concern, not a side issue.
1.5  Social determinants of health — what the nurse sees up close

Figure 5. Layers of influence on health. The nurse observes these determinants at the point of contact (orange centre).
What to see: the person sits at the centre (orange), ringed by widening layers, lifestyle, then social and community networks, then living and working conditions and the wider environment. The nurse meets the person at that orange centre and sees the outer layers pressing in, one patient at a time.
Where the questions live: Q1.5 (nurses already understand SDOH) is the whole diagram, the nurse sees these layers directly; PHM’s job is to aggregate what each nurse sees across the population. Q3.5 (inequity) is what happens when the outer layers are recorded unevenly for different patients.
2.  Nurse: Questions & Sample Responses
Each question names where it lives on a visual (Section 1) and which concept it reroutes to (Master Reroute Anchor Library). Concepts are not re-explained here.
Persona profile
Role lens: Point-of-care assessment, documentation, handoff, follow-up. Often first to notice change; author of the record everyone downstream relies on.
On the maps: clinical / micro level (Fig. 2); acts at Steps 1–2, 4, 5 of the cycle (Fig. 1); works every tier of the pyramid (Fig. 3).
Reality shaping every answer: stretched ratios, documentation-as-liability, floating, moral distress.
Role-inference tells: intake, vitals, charting, handoff, my patient, flag, referral, floated, short-staffed, my license.
Level 1: Foundations
Q1.1  [EVERYDAY→]  “Why am I charting the same intake info the last nurse already entered?”
Real concern: Duplicate documentation, a top workload complaint.
On the map: Steps 1–2 of the cycle (Fig. 1): the record the whole loop depends on.
Reroutes to: [→ INT-DATA]
Reroute A:  That duplication is one of the problems PHM exists to kill. When records connect across the system instead of sitting in silos, the population is described once and the picture follows the person.
Reroute B:  “One connected picture of people and communities” is a named PHM domain, and its absence is exactly why you are typing this twice.
Population tie-back:  Your charting is a shared asset for everyone responsible for that population, not a form you refill.

Q1.2  [PHM-NATIVE]  “What’s the actual difference between population health and PHM?”
On the map: The distinction sits above the whole cycle, population health is the goal, the cycle (Fig. 1) is how PHM pursues it.
Reroutes to: [→ POP-vs-PHM]
Response A:  Population health is what we are improving, a group’s outcomes. PHM is the proactive, data-driven process. Goal versus operating system.
Response B:  Population health spots that food insecurity is high in one area, PHM knows the affected people by name and connects each to support.
Population tie-back:  As a nurse you live at the by-name end, the PHM end of that distinction.

Q1.3  [EVERYDAY→]  “This patient keeps coming back to emerg for the same thing. Why does nothing change?”
Real concern: The frequent-flyer who cycles,  a daily source of moral distress.
On the map: The cycle (Fig. 1) failing to turn,  the person re-enters at the top instead of being caught by Steps 3–4.
Reroutes to: [→ PROACTIVE]  ·  [→ SEGMENT]
Reroute A:  That is the clearest signal PHM looks for. One person cycling back means the system is reacting, not anticipating, and there is usually a whole segment behind them.
Reroute B:  Proactive care uses what we already know to reach people before the emerg visit, which is also what takes that repeat load off your unit.
Population tie-back:  Your one frustrating case is often the visible tip of a population-level gap.

Q1.4  [EVERYDAY→]  “Honestly? This sounds like one more initiative dumped on an already impossible workload.”
Real concern: Workload saturation, the number-one nurse issue in Ontario.
On the map: Care-team well-being on the Quintuple Aim (Fig. 4), the aim that makes your strain a first-class PHM concern.
Reroutes to: [→ PROACTIVE]  ·  [→ INT-DATA]
Reroute A (validate first):  Fair, and PHM is not a parallel task bolted on. Most of what it needs you already do: assess, chart, flag, refer. Where it works, it means fewer avoidable crises landing back on you.
Reroute B (honest limit):  It will not fix staffing, that is a real, separate fight. What it can do is make sure the work you already do to catch people early is not wasted in a system that only reacts.
Population tie-back:  Fewer people hitting crisis unseen is where the crushing workload actually comes from.

Q1.5  [PHM-NATIVE]  “Nurses have understood social determinants for years. What’s PHM adding?”
Real concern: RNAO frames nurses as SDOH experts already; credit that, don’t re-teach it.
On the map: The whole SDOH diagram (Fig. 5) — you see these layers one patient at a time.
Reroutes to: [→ EQUITY/SDOH]  ·  [→ INT-DATA]
Response A:  Exactly right, and PHM leans on that. What you see one patient at a time, PHM aggregates, so the pattern you notice becomes visible across the whole population. It is your bedside knowledge, scaled.
Response B:  The nurse’s own phrase is “meeting needs downstream while trying to move upstream.” PHM connects your downstream work to upstream action.
Population tie-back:  Your shift-level observations become the population’s evidence base.

Level 2: In Practice
Q2.1  [EVERYDAY→]  “I got floated to a unit I don’t know. How am I supposed to know who’s high-priority?”
Real concern: Floating plus competence anxiety, a documented stressor.
On the map: You need the output of Step 3 (Fig. 1, teal),  the stratified priority list, to be legible to any nurse.
Reroutes to: [→ SEGMENT]  ·  [→ RISK-IMPACT]
Reroute A:  That disorientation is what good segmentation and stratification are meant to prevent. A clear picture of who needs what should not live only in the regular nurse’s head.
Reroute B:  What you want floated-in is a stratified list: who is rising-risk, who needs eyes first. That is Step 3, and its point is making priority legible to any nurse.
Population tie-back:  If care collapses when one nurse is moved, that is a system over-relying on individuals instead of a shared population view.

Q2.2  [PHM-NATIVE]  “Risk and impactibility, same thing?”
On the map: The whole Kaiser pyramid (Fig. 3) — impactibility asks not just which tier, but where your action changes the outcome most.
Reroutes to: [→ RISK-IMPACT]
Response A:  Risk is who may have a problem. Impactibility is where our action actually helps. Someone can be high-risk but unlikely to benefit; a medium-risk person may benefit hugely from early support.
Response B (guardrail):  Risk scores inform your judgment, never replace it. You catching the “low-risk but sliding” patient is the system working.
Population tie-back:  Every correction you make sharpens the population picture for the next patient like them.

Q2.3  [EVERYDAY→]  “If I chart everything properly it protects my license. Now you want extra data on top?”
Real concern: Documentation as legal shield  “one audit from a write-up.”
On the map: Steps 1–2 of the cycle (Fig. 1): the same record that protects you is the data the loop runs on.
Reroutes to: [→ INT-DATA]
Reroute A (validate):  Your charting-as-protection instinct is right, and PHM does not compete with it. The same accurate record that protects your license is the data PHM runs on, not extra, your existing charting made to count twice.
Reroute B (honest):  If a PHM design asks for genuinely new fields on top of your legal charting, that is a burden worth flagging, not swallowing.
Population tie-back:  Good documentation protects you individually and builds the population’s picture at the same time.

Q2.4  [PHM-NATIVE]  “Who actually closes a closed-loop referral? If it defaults to the nurse, that’s the workload problem again.”
On the map: Step 4→Step 5 of the cycle (Fig. 1) delivery only counts once the loop is confirmed closed.
Reroutes to: [→ CLOSED-LOOP]
Response A (honest):  It should not all fall to you. The loop can be closed by care coordinators, the referral system, or admin; your part is the accurate capture that makes closing it possible.
Response B:  Unclosed referrals are how people fall through, usually the same populations repeatedly. Closing the loop is an equity mechanism, not admin busywork.
Population tie-back:  Every referral that quietly fails is a gap in the whole population’s care, not just one patient’s.

Level 3: In My Role
Fully nurse-specific. Each answer returns to the population.
Q3.1  [PHM-NATIVE]  “What information do I contribute?”
On the map: The clinical/micro band feeding upward (Fig. 2), and Steps 1–2 of the cycle (Fig. 1).
Reroutes to: [→ INT-DATA]  ·  [→ SEGMENT]
Response A:  You are often the origin of the record, vitals, assessment, social cues, what the patient said. That is the raw material segmentation and stratification run on.
Response B:  You also carry the informal read no form captures, a missed follow-up, a comment about home, a patient who seems “off” compared to last visit. Flagging that is as much a contribution as the vitals.
Population tie-back:  The population picture is only as good as your charting; gaps mean someone gets missed at the population level.

Q3.2  [EVERYDAY→]  “How do I do any of this in the actual minutes I have with a patient?”
Real concern: Time-per-patient is the binding constraint — the nano question in nurse voice.
On the map: Step 4 of the cycle (Fig. 1), inside a single encounter, the point where the whole loop touches one person. [NANO]
Reroutes to: [→ PROACTIVE]
Response A:  You are not running the whole cycle in one visit. Your piece is small and specific: notice, capture accurately, flag when something is off, and that fits the encounter you already have.
Response B:  If a visit genuinely leaves no room for anything extra, that is information too, flag the time pressure itself. A system that never hears about the squeeze can’t be designed to fit it.
Population tie-back:  Those small, faithful moments are what the entire population picture is built from. [NANO: Matt’s moment-of-interaction layer slots in here.]

Q3.3  [PHM-NATIVE]  “Who do I collaborate with, and what happens before and after my part?”
On the map: The micro→meso arrow itself (Fig. 2), and the handoff between cycle steps (Fig. 1).
Reroutes to: [→ SEGMENT]  ·  [→ EVAL-LOOP]
Response A:  Before you: the population is defined and the person identified. Your clinical/micro work feeds professional/meso coordination, primary care, coordinators, social supports. After you: your data feeds assessment, delivery and evaluation.
Response B:  In practice that is usually a short list, not a directory: your unit’s care coordinator, the primary-care team the patient is attached to, and whichever social supports are already engaged. You don’t need to see the whole loop, just the next link.
Population tie-back:  You are one step in a repeating loop; your part only works if it connects back to the whole.

Q3.4  [EVERYDAY→]  ⚠ “I can’t give every patient what they need with these ratios. How is that my failure to fix?”
Real concern: Moral distress and care rationing — nurses quit over this.
On the map: Aiming scarce time up the Kaiser pyramid (Fig. 3), and care-team well-being on the Quintuple Aim (Fig. 4).
Reroutes to: [→ RISK-IMPACT]
Response A:  It is not your failure, and PHM should not pretend otherwise. What it can do is direct scarce time toward where it changes outcomes most, that is what impactibility is for, so rationing becomes a deliberate, defensible allocation.
Response B:  And to be direct: no tool fixes a staffing ratio. What impactibility can do is make the trade-off visible and defensible instead of invisible and arbitrary, different from making it disappear.
Population tie-back:  PHM will not manufacture staff, but it can stop your limited hours from being spread blindly across the population.

Q3.5  [PHM-NATIVE]  “How could my work unintentionally reinforce inequity?”
On the map: The equity node of the Quintuple Aim (Fig. 4), and uneven recording of the SDOH outer layers (Fig. 5).
Reroutes to: [→ EQUITY/SDOH]
Response A:  If documentation is thinner for harder-to-communicate-with patients, language barriers, rushed encounters, those patients go invisible to segmentation and get under-served, even as the numbers look fine.
Response B:  The same risk runs the other way, a patient who presents as “high-need” because they are more articulate or better resourced can crowd out someone equally at-risk who says less. Segmentation is only as fair as the observations feeding it.
Population tie-back:  Equity is a Quintuple Aim outcome; uneven charting bakes inequity into the population view. Proportionate universalism is the counter, more where need is greater. [NANO: equity is won or lost in the individual encounter.]

Q3.6  [PHM-NATIVE]  “How would I know whether my contribution actually worked?”
On the map: Step 5 of the cycle (Fig. 1) your individual signals become the population’s evaluation.
Reroutes to: [→ EVAL-LOOP]  ·  [→ CLOSED-LOOP]
Response A:  Did the referral close? Did the follow-up happen? Did the patient avoid an avoidable ED visit? Those are your individual signals.
Response B:  You often won’t see it directly, the outcome may show up weeks later in someone else’s data. That is normal; Step 5 is a population-level answer built from many individual signals like yours, not a same-visit report card.
Population tie-back:  Evaluation aggregates those signals across the population and feeds the next cycle. Your “did it work?” is one input to the population’s.

Q3.7  [EVERYDAY→]  ⚠ “Is this data going to be used to replace nurses with cheaper staff?”
Real concern: A real, documented fear, RNAO flags RNs replaced by unregulated care providers.
On the map: The data that flows up from the micro level (Fig. 2) the same data can make nursing visible or be misused.
Reroutes to: [→ INT-DATA]  ·  [→ EQUITY/SDOH]
Response A:  Legitimate worry, worth naming. PHM data can be used well or badly, a governance question, not a technical inevitability. Used well, it makes the nurse’s contribution visible: proof that early nursing intervention prevented downstream cost.
Response B:  If that concern isn’t formally answered before the tool ships, it will spread informally on the floor anyway, worth naming as a governance question Southlake commits to answering, not one the tool can talk its way past.
Population tie-back:  The same data that could be misused is the strongest evidence for why skilled nursing at the population’s front line matters.
